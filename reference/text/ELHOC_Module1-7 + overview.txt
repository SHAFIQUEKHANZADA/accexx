Discipline Systems Through an HOC Lens
1. Module Overview
Description
Learners examine and redesign a real discipline routine to build genuine ownership of behavior in students, rather than simply enforcing compliance.
Objective(s)
• Examine and redesign a discipline routine to build ownership instead of just enforcing compliance
Videos / Lessons
Lesson
Format
Time
4.1 What Discipline Systems Actually Teach
Video
~8 min (as scripted)
4.2 Redesigning a Discipline Routine
Video + Worksheet
~8 min (as scripted)
2. Activity — Discipline System Redesign Worksheet
Instructions: Choose one real, current discipline routine and examine what it's actually teaching students, beyond its stated purpose.
The discipline routine I'm examining:
What is this routine actually teaching students about themselves and their behavior — beyond "don't do that again"?
Does this routine build ownership of behavior, or mainly compliance out of fear of consequence? What's the evidence?
My redesign — how does it build genuine ownership while still holding a real standard?
3. Quiz
A short knowledge check (5 questions) on the difference between compliance-based and ownership-based discipline design.
Sample Questions
• 1. A discipline routine that only ever asks "what rule did you break" is primarily building: (a) ownership (b) compliance (c) trust
• 2. A discipline routine that includes "what happened, and what will you do differently" is more likely to build: (a) compliance (b) ownership (c) neither
• 3. True or False: Holding a real standard and building ownership are mutually exclusive.
• 4. What a discipline system teaches is shaped mainly by: (a) its stated purpose (b) what students actually experience when it's applied (c) the length of the policy document
• 5. Which is a sign a discipline system is compliance-based rather than ownership-based: (a) students rarely repeat the same behavior because they understand why it mattered (b) students avoid the behavior mainly to avoid getting caught (c) students help design the consequences
Followed by submission of the completed Discipline System Redesign Worksheet above.

Fail

An assessment is made but stays diplomatic rather than honest

Genuine ownership mechanism

Some ownership elements included but consequence still dominates
The redesign remains purely consequence-based
Standard maintained

6.2 Auditing Policy Against Stated Values
Video + Worksheet
~8 min (as scripted)
2. Activity — Governance & Policy Alignment Audit
Instructions: Choose two real school policies or governance structures and audit each against the school's stated values.
Policy/Structure 1:
What does this policy actually do, and to whom?
Does it reinforce or undermine the school's stated values? How?
Policy/Structure 2:
What does this policy actually do, and to whom?
Does it reinforce or undermine the school's stated values? How?
Of the two, which represents the higher-leverage priority to address, and why?
3. Quiz
A short knowledge check (5 questions) on identifying genuine policy misalignment.
Sample Questions
• 1. A school that states it values "every family's voice" but has no real mechanism for non-English-speaking parents to participate in meetings shows: (a) strong alignment (b) misalignment (c) an unrelated issue
• 2. Auditing governance for alignment means asking: (a) is this policy legally compliant (b) does this policy's real effect match the stated value (c) is this policy popular
• 3. True or False: A policy can be well-intentioned and still misaligned with stated values in practice.
• 4. When choosing which misalignment to prioritize, the best criterion is generally: (a) whichever is easiest to fix (b) whichever affects the most people most directly (c) whichever was noticed first
• 5. Governance structures shape culture mainly through: (a) their written intent (b) who actually has voice and influence in practice (c) how long they've existed
Followed by submission of the completed Governance & Policy Alignment Audit above.
