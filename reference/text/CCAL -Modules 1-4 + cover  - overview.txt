Diagnosing Culture: What's Really Rewarded
1. Module Overview
Description
Learners map what's genuinely rewarded, tolerated, and punished in a real organization — the gap between the culture written on the wall and the culture people actually live inside every day.
Objective(s)
• Map what's actually rewarded, tolerated, and punished in a real organization
• Distinguish stated values from lived culture

2. Activity — Culture Diagnostic Worksheet
Instructions: Use this worksheet to map the real, lived culture of an organization you know well — not the stated values on the website, but what actually happens.
The organization I'm mapping:
What is genuinely rewarded here? (Promotions, praise, attention — be specific about real incidents.)
What is tolerated, even though it contradicts the stated values? (Behavior nobody stops.)
What is actually punished — formally or informally?
Where is the biggest gap between what's stated and what's lived?
3. Assignment
Submit the completed worksheet along with a short-written reflection (150–200 words) identifying the single biggest gap between the organization's stated culture and its lived culture, and why you think that gap exists.

Examples are plausible but vague or under-detailed
No real examples given; purely generic statements
Stated vs. lived distinction
Clearly and accurately separates what's written from what's actually practiced
The distinction is gestured at but blurred in places
Stated and lived culture are not meaningfully distinguished
Genuine gap identified
Names a real, non-obvious gap with a plausible explanation for why it exists
Names a gap but the explanation is thin or generic
No real gap identified, or it's a surface-level observation

Instructions: List the organizational systems that most shape day-to-day behavior, and rate each one against your Culture Blueprint from Module 2.

Of everything audited, which one system is the highest-leverage priority to fix first, and why?
3. Quiz
A short knowledge check (5 questions) on identifying genuine system misalignment versus surface-level mismatches.
Questions
• 1. A hiring process that says it values collaboration but scores candidates almost entirely on individual technical tests is an example of: (a) strong alignment (b) a misaligned system (c) an unrelated system
• 2. When prioritizing which system to fix first, the best criterion is: (a) whichever is easiest to change (b) whichever touches the most people most often (c) whichever was audited first
• 3. True or False: A single symbolic gesture (like a poster) can meaningfully realign a system on its own.
• 4. Which is a real system, in the sense this module means it: (a) "our culture" broadly (b) the promotion criteria actually used in review meetings (c) the mission statement
• 5. Why audit multiple systems rather than just one? (a) it looks more thorough (b) misalignment is often concentrated in a specific system that isn't obvious until compared to others (c) it isn't necessary to audit more than one
Followed by submission of the completed Systems Alignment Audit above, including your identified top priority.
